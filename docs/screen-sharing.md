# Sharing a screen

[Documentation](README.md)

`src/Yaparr.Screen` shares one monitor or one window picked from a list (on Windows it uses Windows Graphics Capture through FFmpeg 8.1 or newer, so browsers and games come through and windows on top do not matter; on Linux see [Linux](#linux) below: monitors and windows on X11, what the desktop's picker offers on Wayland) with a room, and shows a share on any system. It needs FFmpeg, which it runs as a separate program: it looks in the `YAPARR_FFMPEG` variable, next to the app, in the app's own downloaded copy (`FfmpegInstaller`, which verifies a pinned file by its hash) and on the PATH. The picture is encoded as H.264 on the graphics card (NVIDIA's NVENC, AMD's AMF or Intel's Quick Sync, tried in that order) when the computer has one that works, and as VP8 in software otherwise, and travels through the same tree as any stream, so a big room does not load the sharer. In the Desktop app a room has a share-screen button in the voice bar, shown while you are in the room's voice channel (or while another of your linked devices is: see [Sharing from a linked device](#sharing-from-a-linked-device)) and gone when you leave it (pick a monitor or a window, and a quality) and a list of the shares in the room (shown, like the Share button, only while you are in voice) with a Watch button that opens the picture. The picture is shown either inside the app over the chat or in a window of its own: **Pop out** and **Show in the app** (the button under the picture) move it between the two without restarting the stream, the last choice is remembered for the next share, and only one picture sits over the chat at a time (a second one goes to its own window). The picture over the chat shows while you look at its room and keeps playing when you look elsewhere; if FFmpeg is not on the computer the app asks before downloading it. `tools/Yaparr.ScreenDemo` shares this PC's screen to a second node in the same process and reports the codec, the first-picture time, bitrate and processor use (`Yaparr.ScreenDemo --seconds 20 --monitor 0 --codec auto --save picture.bmp`).

While you share, a small readout window shows the encoder, frames per second, key frames, the watchers connected directly to you, and who is watching (everyone in the tree, by name, one entry per person) and, after ten seconds, who keeps asking to join without getting in, with a pointer to the connection guide (`MediaSource.Viewers` and `MediaSource.Unreached`, from the sharer's picture of the tree and the join requests it has seen). The `.deb` recommends the system `ffmpeg` package, which the viewer uses on Linux.

**Preview.** While you share, **What viewers see** shows above the readout, in the People panel: the frames that are being sent, decoded again here (`ScreenSharer.FrameEncoded` into a `ScreenViewer` with a `PreviewSize` through `LocalFrames`) and scaled down to at most 640x360 by FFmpeg, so it is the real picture with its quality and blur, not a copy of the capture. It keeps about 15 pictures a second (`YAPARR_PREVIEW_FPS` changes that; 1 or 2 means key frames only). Showing more than the key frames means decoding every frame, since a frame cannot be decoded without the ones before it, and that is what costs: the first version decoded all of them in software and drew them at full size, and cost more than the whole share (this program 138% of a core with the preview open, 10% with it hidden). Now the card decodes through Direct3D 12 and scales there too (`scale_d3d12`), so that only the small picture is copied back to memory: measured with FFmpeg on two 1080p streams, 0.3 to 0.6 s of processor time for 250 to 480 frames, against 1.5 to 2.6 s when the whole frame is copied back and scaled by the processor (Direct3D 11, which has no scaling filter that works: `scale_d3d11` fails to create its texture on the card tried) and 2 to 4 s in software. Where FFmpeg or the card cannot, the preview falls back to Direct3D 11, then to the processor, and the log's sharing line says which it is using and what did not work (`preview decoder N% (on the card through Direct3D 12)`). Only the pictures kept are converted and drawn. With key frames only (`YAPARR_PREVIEW_FPS=1`) the decoder runs on one thread and gets each key frame twice (with several threads, FFmpeg holds a picture back until the next goes in), and the frames in between are not even copied. It costs a second decoder process while it is open, so it only runs while it is shown (it stops when you look at another room, and with **Hide**). Sharing a window starts it open; sharing a whole monitor starts it closed, because the preview would show itself inside the picture (**Show** opens it to check the quality, and it closes again when you change to a monitor). The log's per-minute sharing line and the totals when a share stops say how much processor time the preview's decoder used (`preview decoder N%`), which the figure for this program does not include.

The distribution is built: `StartMedia`, `WatchMedia` and the tree behind them pass signed packets from a sharer to a whole room ([Streams and large rooms](streams-and-large-rooms.md)), with a cache of the packets since the last key for late viewers.

## What is built

First layer built (`src/Yaparr.Screen`, `tools/Yaparr.ScreenDemo`): FFmpeg runs as a separate program; capture is Windows Graphics Capture through FFmpeg's `gfxcapture` filter (FFmpeg 8.1 or newer: 8.0 does not have the filter) of one monitor (`Displays.List`) or one window (`ShareWindows.List`), encoded as H.264 on an NVIDIA graphics card (`h264_nvenc`: low-latency, no B-frames, variable rate, a delimiter before every picture, read back by `H264Reader`) when FFmpeg can start it, otherwise as VP8 (libvpx, realtime) in IVF in software, and the choice is made by trying the card first (`ScreenCodec.Auto`: the sharer starts with the card and falls back to VP8 if FFmpeg stops before the first picture), sent frame by frame through the tree; a viewer reads the codec from the key frame; the viewer feeds the frames to a second FFmpeg that decodes to NV12, which `Nv12` turns into BGRA pictures with vector instructions (FFmpeg's own conversion cost about as much as the decoding; 1080p60 end to end in `tools/Yaparr.ScreenDemo` went from about 145% to 60-80% of one core on the desktop). `ScreenOptions.GpuScale` (`--gpu-scale`, NVIDIA H.264, monitors only) has `gfxcapture` scale on the card and hands the frames straight to NVENC, so they never come down to system memory; `scale_d3d11` cannot be used for this (it fails to create its output textures on `gfxcapture` frames). `ScreenViewer(gpuDecode: true)` (`--gpu-decode`) decodes H.264 on the card through Direct3D 11 with a fall back to the processor, but is off by default: it used about 100% of a core against 60-80% in software on the desktop. Measured on this PC (primary monitor 1920x1080 scaled to 1280x720, 10 fps, 800 kbit/s asked): first picture after 2.3 s, nothing dropped or lost, 935 kbit/s on average (VP8 overshoots on a busy screen), about 28% of one core for the encoder, the decoder and the demo together. The LGPL build used has libvpx, `h264_nvenc`, `h264_amf`, `h264_qsv` and `gfxcapture`. FFmpeg is downloaded on first use into %LOCALAPPDATA%\Yaparrfmpeg by `FfmpegInstaller` (hash checked, licence and source note kept next to it); the pinned file is meant to be copied to a Yaparr release tagged `ffmpeg-1`, which does not exist yet. The Desktop app has the first version of the interface (Share screen with a monitor and quality picker, a list of shares with Watch, the viewer, the download question); it builds and starts but has not been used with a second person or looked at closely.

Compared on this PC: the old screen-copying capture (gdigrab) returned a nearly black picture for a hardware-accelerated browser window while Graphics Capture returned its content; a window with another window on top was captured correctly by Graphics Capture (and by gdigrab's window mode); for a monitor Graphics Capture used about 40% of the processor time. gdigrab was removed to keep the app small: sharing all monitors as one picture is gone (share one monitor), and an FFmpeg older than 8.1 is not supported. A minimised window stops updating, and a resized window keeps its shape inside its first size (`resize_mode=scale_aspect`); neither has been tried with viewers.

## Sharing from a linked device

A share belongs to a voice channel, and only people in that channel see it listed. A device that is not in the call can still share into it while another device of the same account is in it: you join voice on your desktop and share from your laptop, and keep talking on the desktop.

- `ChatRoom.ScreenShareChannel` is the channel a share from this device goes to: the one this device is in, or else the one another of your linked devices is in (found from the voice announcements that device makes to the room like anyone in a call, `ChatNode.ChannelOfMyOtherDevice`). `ChatRoom.CanShareScreen` is true when there is one. `ScreenSharer.Start` announces the share in that channel.
- With none of your devices in a voice channel there is nothing to share into: the Share button is not shown, and the app says to join voice on one of your devices if it is asked anyway.
- The desktop app shows the button on a device that is not in the call as soon as a linked one joins. A share from your other device shows in the call's list as yours, without a Watch button (the share is not shown back to you, which would loop the picture and the sound).
- A share that was started this way ends by itself when none of your devices has been in that channel for about twelve seconds (the announcements come every few seconds and can be late), with a note in the room. A device that shares from its own call still stops when it leaves it.
- Viewers need nothing new. The share is announced by the laptop's own key, and everyone already knows it belongs to your account (the same way messages from your other devices are recognised).
- A phone with Android 14 or newer shares by the same rule (`ChatRoom.ScreenShareChannel`): see [Sharing the screen](android-app.md#sharing-the-screen). It sends the same kind of stream (H.264, Annex B, parameter sets in front of each key frame) and the same sound stream (`ScreenSharer.SoundKind`), so the computer's viewer shows and plays it without any change; and a phone can watch the computer's share, H.264 or VP8, with its own decoder (see [Watching a screen](android-app.md#watching-a-screen)).

## Linux

Sharing works on Linux under X11 and under Wayland, with the system FFmpeg (install it with your package manager; the `.deb` recommends it and the other programs below). The picture goes through the same tree and viewer as on Windows. It is encoded as H.264 on NVIDIA's `h264_nvenc` where the card has it, then on Intel or AMD graphics through VA-API (`h264_vaapi`, on the first `/dev/dri/renderD*` or the device named in `YAPARR_VAAPI_DEVICE`), and as VP8 in software otherwise. The command lines are built by `FfmpegArgs` and checked in `LinuxScreenTests`, which also runs on Linux in the Test workflow.

**In this program (Linux).** Where FFmpeg's shared libraries are there (the Flatpak carries them in `/app/lib/yaparr-ffmpeg`; otherwise the app downloads the Yaparr.FFmpeg build, which has them next to its `ffmpeg`, the first time a share needs them), H.264 is encoded inside Yaparr, as on Windows (`InProcessEncoder`): on NVIDIA's `h264_nvenc` with NV12 pictures, or on an Intel or AMD card through VA-API (`h264_vaapi`: the render node is opened, each NV12 picture is uploaded into a picture on the card, and that is encoded). On X11 a separate FFmpeg still captures (`x11grab`) and writes raw NV12 to a pipe; on Wayland there is no FFmpeg in the share at all: GStreamer scales the desktop's stream and writes NV12 pictures of the size to send, `PaceAsync` hands them on at the frame rate through a pipe, and the encoder takes each one as it is (`RunInProcessPortalCoreAsync`; `LinuxScreenTests` runs it with a stand-in `gst-launch-1.0`). What it gives is what the Windows share has: a viewer's request for a key frame is answered on the next picture, and adaptive quality (`docs/adaptive-quality.md`): NVIDIA's encoder takes a new bitrate while it runs (`LiveBitrate`), VA-API's cannot, so a new bitrate there, like a new frame rate or size, ends the run and the capture program and encoder are started again at the new level (about a second without a picture, then a key frame). It is the default on Linux (`YAPARR_INPROCESS=0` turns it off); without the libraries, or where the encoder cannot start, the share is as it was. Written against the NVIDIA path and tested on it under WSL (`InProcessEncoderTests`); the VA-API path has not run on a real Intel or AMD card (WSL has none), only up to the point where it says plainly that no card is there.

**X11.** A monitor or a window is copied with FFmpeg's `x11grab`. The monitors are listed with `xrandr --listmonitors` and the windows with `xprop` and `xwininfo` (the `x11-xserver-utils` and `x11-utils` packages; panels, menus and minimised windows are left out, see `X11Windows`). A window is grabbed by its id; when it gets smaller, `x11grab` stops and FFmpeg is started again at the new size (viewers pick the new size up at the next key frame), and when it gets bigger, only the area it had when sharing began is shared, as on Windows (where a resized window keeps its shape inside its first size). The share dialog says which of the programs above are missing; whether a window that another one covers comes out right depends on the desktop having a compositor, which current desktops do.

**A window chosen in the desktop's picker, on X11.** `x11grab` stops when a shared window gets smaller (the size it asks X for is fixed when it opens), so the capture is started again at the new size, which costs a second without a picture. Where the session also has the desktop's ScreenCast portal (GNOME and KDE have it on X11 as well) and GStreamer, the share dialog offers one more choice, "A window, chosen in the desktop's picker" (`ScreenCapture.OffersPortalWindowOnX11`, found out once). That share is the Wayland path (the desktop's PipeWire stream, GStreamer, the encoder in this program): the desktop keeps handing over the window's pictures when it is resized or covered, and GStreamer scales them into the size that was set, with black borders where the shape differs (its `videoscale` adds them by default), so nothing restarts. The price: the desktop asks which window every time, GStreamer is needed, and the desktop's picker does not say which program the window belongs to, so the sound is the computer's (everything except Yaparr) unless the person names a program in the dialog (a sound choice appears when this window choice is selected, listing the programs that are playing now), as on Wayland. Not tried on a real X11 desktop with a portal, only with the stand-in GStreamer of `LinuxScreenTests`.

**Wayland** (the default on current GNOME and KDE). A program may not copy the screen itself there, nor list the windows: it asks the desktop's ScreenCast portal (xdg-desktop-portal, over D-Bus, `PortalScreenCast`), the desktop shows its own picker, and the answer is a PipeWire stream. FFmpeg cannot read that, so GStreamer's `gst-launch-1.0` does (`pipewiresrc`, on the connection the portal handed over, which the child program inherits), scales the pictures to the size to send, steadies the frame rate, and writes them as raw video into FFmpeg, which encodes them (`PortalPipeline`). It needs `gstreamer1.0-tools` and `gstreamer1.0-pipewire`; when they are missing the app says so before the desktop asks what to share. In the app the share dialog offers "A monitor" or "A window" and the desktop does the choosing; stopping the share from the desktop's own indicator ends it here too. The portal, D-Bus and GStreamer parts were run against a stand-in portal and a PipeWire test source in a headless session (the real wlroots portal backend, `xdg-desktop-portal-wlr`, answered the requests but could not capture the virtual output there), not yet on a real GNOME or KDE desktop.

**Sound** on Linux comes from the sound server's streams, listed with `pactl` and recorded with `parec --monitor-stream`, through the PulseAudio interface that PulseAudio and PipeWire both offer (the `pulseaudio-utils` package, `PulseAppCapture`). A shared **X11 window** shares all the streams of its own program (and the programs it started), mixed together; the program is found by process, by its file name and by the window's class names (`WM_CLASS`), which is what a sandboxed program or a browser is known by. A shared **monitor** shares every stream that is playing except Yaparr's own (and the programs it started), mixed together, so the voice chat is not sent back to the people in it. On **Wayland** the portal does not say which program a window belongs to, so the share is the same as a monitor's, but the share dialog can name one program instead (the ones playing sound at that moment are listed; `ScreenOptions.SoundProgram`). New streams are picked up within a second.

The **Flatpak** carries everything above: its own FFmpeg with `x11grab`, VP8, NVIDIA and VA-API H.264 (the graphics card's encoder comes from the host's drivers, so on a computer without a usable card the next encoder is tried), `xrandr`, `xprop` and `xwininfo` built into it, and `pactl`, `parec` and GStreamer's PipeWire source from the runtime. On Wayland the sandbox may always use the ScreenCast portal, and the sound goes through the PulseAudio socket it already has. The sandbox is also given the Wayland socket (`--socket=wayland`), only so that the app can tell it is on Wayland: a Flatpak is not given `XDG_SESSION_TYPE`, and `WAYLAND_DISPLAY` only exists with that socket. Without it a Wayland desktop looked like X11, the share copied the picture from XWayland, and a viewer saw the first picture and nothing after it (fixed after a friend's report); the window itself is still drawn through X11. It was built and run on Ubuntu under WSL (a real X11 capture and VP8 encode in the sandbox; the app starts), not yet on a real desktop with the portal.

Not there yet: a real session on GNOME and KDE.

## Design

- Sender: FFmpeg's Windows Graphics Capture filter (`gfxcapture`) captures and FFmpeg encodes. Use a hardware H.264 encoder (NVENC, AMF, Quick Sync) when the machine has one, otherwise VP8 or VP9 in software; pick with a short test at the start.
- Transport: the encoded packets go over an unreliable channel, split into datagrams of about 1100 bytes with sequence numbers, and a keyframe is requested when a viewer joins or loses packets.
- Sender, in this program by default (Windows): a share on an NVIDIA card with FFmpeg's libraries next to the app (they come in the same package as `ffmpeg.exe`, see the build in [Yaparr.FFmpeg](https://github.com/zburtle/Yaparr.FFmpeg); `FfmpegLibraries` looks in the `ffmpeg` folder next to the app, in the folder of the FFmpeg that was installed, and in `YAPARR_FFMPEG_LIBS`) captures, scales and encodes inside the program with the pictures kept on the graphics card (the two bullets below). Anything else (another card or none, VP8 or AV1, Linux, libraries that are missing, too old, or not LGPL, a minimised window whose size is unknown, a card that refuses) is a share as before, with a separate FFmpeg, and the log says which and why. `YAPARR_INPROCESS` changes it: `0` or `off` for a separate FFmpeg only, `1` for the encoder alone, `capture-cpu` for capture here with the pictures copied to memory, `capture` for the default spelled out. Running native code inside the program means a crash in it takes the program along (a separate FFmpeg crashes alone), so `InProcessGuard` leaves a mark when such a share starts and takes it away when it ends; a mark found at the next start means the program ended in the middle of one, and after two of those in a row the default goes back to a separate FFmpeg until a share ends well (`YAPARR_INPROCESS=capture` asks for it regardless, and the log says when the guard has turned it off). A window's picture is its client area, without the title bar and the borders, which is what the capture itself makes of it; the size handed to the card-side scaling is worked out from that when sharing begins.
- Sender, in-process encoder (`YAPARR_INPROCESS=1` asks for this alone): FFmpeg's libraries (a folder next to the app, or the one `YAPARR_FFMPEG_LIBS` names; LGPL builds only) encode H.264 on NVIDIA's encoder inside the app, on Windows and X11. FFmpeg still captures and scales, as a separate program, and hands over raw NV12 pictures on a pipe, so the pictures come down to system memory and `GpuScale` is not used. What it gives: a viewer's request for a key frame (`MediaSource.KeyFrameRequested`) is answered on the very next picture (`KeyFramePacer` forces at most one per 500 ms, keeps a request that comes sooner and answers it when the gap has passed), and each packet comes out as soon as it is made (`delay` 0), where NVENC otherwise holds two pictures back. It is not the default and the libraries are not shipped yet (issue #225); if they are missing or the encoder cannot start, the share falls back to a separate FFmpeg. The sharing panel ("in this program", and the count in its tooltip) and the share's report say that it is in use and how many key frames were made because a viewer asked. To try it from Visual Studio: run `pwsh tools\Get-FfmpegLibs.ps1` once (it fetches an LGPL shared build into `artifacts\ffmpeg-libs`, which a Debug build of the desktop app copies next to the program), then start the "Yaparr (your profile, in-process encoder)" launch profile.
- Sender, in-process capture (Windows): the capture runs inside the program as well (`InProcessCapture`): the same filter chain as the command line (Graphics Capture, copy back to memory, scale to fit, NV12) built as a filter graph with FFmpeg's libraries, whose pictures go to the in-process encoder as they are (the encoder reads them from FFmpeg's memory, so no pipe, no child process and no copy). The capture hands over a picture when the screen changes, so the share's own clock decides when a picture is encoded and the last one is repeated when there is none new (counted as "pictures repeated"): the encoder gets an even 60 a second, where the command-line FFmpeg tops the stream up with copies that arrive in bursts. It needs libraries with the Graphics Capture fixes of October 2026 (the capture's `dup_frames` option; `tools/Get-FfmpegLibs.ps1 -Master` fetches such a build for development; Yaparr's own build of the libraries from the pinned commit is still to come), and where they are missing the share says so ("too old") and goes on with the encoder here and a separate FFmpeg capturing. The log and the diagnostics then say "capture in this program" instead of the capture FFmpeg's processor time, which is part of this program's own. With `YAPARR_INPROCESS=capture` the pictures also stay on the graphics card: Graphics Capture scales them there (`width` and `height`, worked out from the monitor's size or the window's visible frame when sharing begins, the shape kept, even numbers, never enlarged), the filter graph ends at the capture, and NVENC reads the Direct3D 11 pictures as they are and converts them itself, so nothing is copied to memory and converted on the processor. Measured on a 1080p60 share at 6000 kbit/s, delivering 59 pictures a second: 65% of a core in this program with the pictures copied to memory (10.9 to 11.4 ms of processor time per picture sent), 5 to 7% with them kept on the card (0.9 to 1.2 ms), against 62% of a core for the separate FFmpeg. Where the size is not known (a minimised window) or the card refuses, the share goes on with the copy to memory and says why; `YAPARR_INPROCESS=capture-cpu` is the copy to memory only, for comparing. A window that changes shape while shared keeps its first size, with bars where the shape no longer fits. The program also asks Windows for a 1 ms timer for as long as such a share lasts, so that waits are as short as asked. Windows only so far; Linux (X11 and Wayland) is still the capture FFmpeg and GStreamer.
- Sender, adaptive quality in shadow mode (#225 stage 3, [design](adaptive-quality.md)): viewers judge their own picture over ten seconds (`ViewerQualityMeter`: under nine in ten pictures shown, or a decoder that drops frames, is trouble) and send one small report every two seconds up the tree, which each relay folds with its children's (the `quality` control operation, counts only; older versions ignore it, and a root counts only the viewers whose reports reach it, so no report is no information). `MediaSource.Room` at the root says how many reported and how many are in trouble. `ScreenSharer` runs `QualityPolicy` on it and on its own link once a second and writes what it would do to the log (`adaptive quality (shadow mode, nothing is changed): would lower to ...`) and its state to the sharing line and the report; what it does about it is in [the design](adaptive-quality.md): the steps down the presets of the share dialog (1080p60, 1080p30, 720p30) are applied when the encoder runs in this program and the share dialog's setting is on (the default); a step that changes the frame rate or size builds the capture and encoder again. Repairs are capped at a quarter of the stream, and the stream a person shares is not charged to what the device forwards for others. One viewer in trouble among three or more never lowers it; half the room for ten seconds does, a quiet half minute raises it again, and a step up that is followed by trouble makes the next wait twice as long.
- Sender diagnostics: when a share starts the log says what was asked for (codec, encoder, scaling, key interval, in-process or not), which libraries were loaded (path, versions, licence) and, once an encoder runs, which one it really is and how it was set up (the options, or the FFmpeg command line); it says again if another encoder takes over, with the reason the first was not used. Every minute while sharing it writes one `call quality: sharing:` line, whether or not anything is wrong: frame rate and bitrate, key frames (how many, how many were made because a viewer asked, their size against the other frames', what became of the requests and how long they waited), the encoder's time per picture and how many pictures took longer than there is time for, the wait for the capture, the longest stall, packets sent again and dropped, who is watching and over which path, the processor used by this program and the capture program, memory, garbage collections and their pauses, how many times the capture program was restarted, and what NVIDIA's `nvidia-smi` says about the card (how busy it and its video encoder are, memory, temperature, clock, power, encoder sessions). A key frame made because a viewer asked is logged when it goes out, with the delay and the size. When the share ends the line has the totals. The "Copy sharing diagnostics" button (next to "Copy diagnostics") includes all of it (`ScreenSharer.Describe`).
- Viewer diagnostics: every minute while watching, the log gets a `call quality: watching` line (marked "(a problem)" when something is wrong) with the frames arriving, pictures drawn, frames lost and skipped, and what became of the packets: how many came, how many were missing when a later one arrived, and what then happened to those (they turned up by themselves a moment later, which is a link that reorders; they were repaired after being asked for, which is loss that resending fixes; they never came, which is loss it did not), how many resend requests were made for how many packets, and how many gaps were too big to ask about. The same counts are in "Copy sharing diagnostics". They tell a link that loses packets from one that only delivers them late, and a viewer that asks for too much from one that asks for too little. The figures also count the copies refused (a packet already taken, or from too far back). A receiver once remembered only the last 64 packet numbers (`SeqWindow`), about a tenth of a second at 6 Mbit/s, and refused every repair that arrived later than that as a duplicate: the first real logs of a Wi-Fi viewer showed about 6,000 packets sent again a minute and about 250 taken. It now remembers 4,096 (about six seconds at 6 Mbit/s, well past the second and a half a viewer keeps asking), and in a test at 8 Mbit/s with one packet in twenty lost, repairs taken went from 37% of the gaps (599 copies refused) to all of them.
- Viewer: FFmpeg decodes to raw frames drawn in a viewer tile. Pictures wait in a queue of a few and are drawn on a steady clock (`PlayoutQueue`, `PlayoutClock`) whose pace is the number of pictures that arrived in the last second over the time they took, so bunched arrivals (jitter, or a burst after the sender stalled) do not slow it. An earlier clock averaged the gaps between arrivals after lifting each to at least 15 ms, which at 60 pictures a second ran too slow whenever arrivals were bunched: a viewer drew 49 of the 60 pictures that arrived (the rest counted as "too late to show") with nothing lost, and at 120 a second about half. Windows bundles it; the `.deb` recommends the system `ffmpeg` package. Android can only watch (its own decoders handle both codecs).
- A share is announced like a voice beacon (who, which screen or window, size, codec), and nothing is sent to anyone who has not clicked Watch. A blocked person's share is never shown.

## FFmpeg and the encoders

- **The program.** Yaparr builds its own FFmpeg: a static `ffmpeg.exe` of about 9.5 MB with only what screen sharing uses (and, for Linux x64 and arm64, an `ffmpeg` of about 5 MB with libvpx and the X11 libraries linked in, which needs only the C library and libva from the system), under the LGPL, made by the build in the [Yaparr.FFmpeg](https://github.com/zburtle/Yaparr.FFmpeg) repository and published as a release (`ffmpeg-N`) with the exact FFmpeg source, the licences of what is built in, and checksums. It replaced a 175 MB general-purpose build. See [Development](development.md#the-ffmpeg-build).
- **Graphics card encoders.** Auto tries NVIDIA, then AMD, then Intel, and VP8 in software last; each one that cannot start fails within about half a second before the next is tried. On an Intel laptop (1366x768, 15 frames a second) the Intel encoder sent about 30% less than VP8 at about the same processor use, and the first picture took about 0.6 s instead of 0.3 s. The AMD encoder has been checked only at the command line level (the options exist in the encoder), not on an AMD card.
- **Patents.** VP8 has a royalty-free patent grant. H.264 does not; Yaparr decodes it in software with FFmpeg's decoder and encodes it only on the user's own graphics card. The AVC/H.264 Patent Portfolio License is free for the first 100,000 units a year per company and is silent on free downloads and open-source software; see the [backlog](../BACKLOG.md).

## AV1

AV1 compresses a screen better than H.264 (the same sharpness for noticeably fewer bits, which matters on a thin upload), but it is only an option, chosen in the share dialog under **Video format**, and **Automatic** stays H.264 and then VP8. Two reasons: only graphics cards encode it live (NVIDIA RTX 40 and newer through `av1_nvenc`, AMD RX 7000 and newer through `av1_amf` or VA-API, Intel Arc and newer through `av1_qsv` or VA-API; software AV1 cannot keep up with a screen at 30 or 60 frames a second), and every viewer needs a decoder for it, which an old Yaparr, an FFmpeg without dav1d or a phone without an AV1 decoder does not have, so one sharer's choice must not leave a room's viewers with a black picture.

- **What is sent.** One temporal unit per frame, in IVF like VP8 (FFmpeg's IVF writer puts a temporal delimiter in front of each unit). The viewer reads the codec off the key frame (`ScreenCodecs.TryGetKeyFrame`): a unit that starts with a temporal delimiter and holds a sequence header. A key frame is the unit with a sequence header (`Av1.IsKeyFrame`), whose maximum frame size is the picture size (`Av1.TryGetSize`, which reads the header by hand, so no FFmpeg is needed to know it). Nothing is sent before the first one.
- **What is tried.** Asked for AV1, the sharer tries it on each graphics card in turn (`ScreenSharer.Plan`), and then does what Automatic does (H.264 on each card, VP8 in software), so a computer with no AV1 card still shares; the readout and **Copy sharing diagnostics** say which one it ended up with and why the others were not used.
- **The start-up check.** A little after the app starts (once FFmpeg is on the computer), `Av1Support` asks FFmpeg for a tiny AV1 picture from each card encoder in the background and reads what comes out: the card must make a key frame that carries a readable sequence header, which is exactly what a viewer needs, so this also settles the question below on every maker's card the first time it runs. It also looks for a software decoder (libdav1d or libaom) in FFmpeg. The answer is in the log (`AV1 check: ...`) and **Copy sharing diagnostics** (the "AV1" line). When no card passes, the share dialog shows **AV1** greyed out with the reasons instead of offering a choice that would end in H.264 anyway; while the check has not finished, or FFmpeg was only just downloaded, AV1 stays available. It is not repeated until the app is started again.
- **Watching.** The viewer decodes in software with FFmpeg's libdav1d; on a phone the system decoder is used (`video/av01`, which Android has in software from version 10, and on newer phones in hardware), and a phone that has none says so instead of showing nothing. The Windows FFmpeg (the [Yaparr.FFmpeg](https://github.com/zburtle/Yaparr.FFmpeg) build) therefore has to have `libdav1d` and the AV1 encoders in it before a shipped Yaparr can use AV1: the pinned copy in `FfmpegPackage` does not have either yet, and a share started with it falls back to H.264 as above. The Flatpak manifest asks for them (the runtime has dav1d).
- **Not checked yet on a real card.** The development PC has an RTX 3060, which does not encode AV1, so the encoder options (checked as text in `Av1Tests`) and, above all, whether each maker's encoder puts a sequence header in front of every key frame on its own have not been run. If one does not, the share would start at no key frame and send nothing: that is the first thing to look at on a real card.

## Bursts

A key frame is several times the size of an ordinary frame, and on a home connection a burst fills the router's queue, where voice packets wait behind it: voices turn robotic at the same moment frames drop. So key frames were two seconds apart (`ScreenOptions.KeyFrameSeconds`; before that one, since loss is repaired by replaying from the packet cache, not by a new key frame). They are one second apart again, as an experiment: a viewer that loses frames throws everything away until the next key frame, and on a friend's cable line at 60 fps that wait was most of the lag. If voices turn robotic while sharing, this is the first thing to put back to 2 and a frame is sent out at three times the stream's bitrate instead of four (`MediaSource.SendFrameAsync`). The share dialog starts at the best quality that the measured upload can send to three people at once. Once the upload has been measured, the pace of a frame also stays within four fifths of it, shared between the viewers the sharer sends to directly, and never under a fifth more than the stream itself (`UploadPlan.FramePaceBytesPerSecond`): a friend's 6 Mbit/s share went out at 18 Mbit/s from a line measured at 7.6 Mbit/s, which filled the router's queue and lost packets by the thousand (thousands of packets sent again a minute, waits over a second), where Discord, which paces to the bandwidth it measures, was fine on the same line. A fast line, and a stream far below the line (2 Mbit/s on 9), keep the 3x pace.

## Watching: what the log and the diagnostics say

The sharer's side has long been in the call quality log and **Copy sharing diagnostics** ("Your share"). The viewer's side is there too (`WatchFigures`, from `ScreenViewControl.Figures`): each minute the call quality log writes a `call quality: watching …` line for a screen that is not going well (frames lost, too late or dropped by the decoder, fewer than ten pictures a second drawn or far fewer drawn than arrive, a decoder that is behind, or no path to the sharer), with the codec and picture size, the frames arriving a second, the bitrate, the frames skipped while waiting for a key frame (after a loss the viewer throws away everything until the next key frame, which `frames lost` does not count), the pictures drawn, who the stream comes from and how that connection runs. **Copy sharing diagnostics** has a "Screens you watch" section with the figures for the whole watch, and for the last three that ended. Together with the sharer's report this says on which side of the connection a lagging share loses its pictures. The sharer's first minute used to read as a burst of frames (the share's whole count was divided by one second); it is now only taken as a starting point.

## A burst of pictures from the portal

On a KDE desktop (Flatpak, Wayland) a share sent 7,080 "frames" in 18 seconds for a 60 frames a second share, and in another run an average of 3,900 frames a second at 109 Mbit/s for a share asked to send 2 Mbit/s: the encoder was fed many times the pictures it should have, mostly copies of one picture, so viewers were flooded with data and showed the first picture and nothing after it. How GStreamer came to hand over so many is not proven (`videorate` makes up the pictures between time zero and the first one when the stream's first picture is stamped much later, which fits the size of the burst), so the picture is now paced in two places: `videorate` runs with `skip-to-first=true max-duplication-time=500000000` (no making up of the time before the first picture or of a long gap), and `ScreenSharer.PaceAsync` reads the raw pictures GStreamer writes and gives FFmpeg exactly the frame rate: the newest picture at each tick (a still screen gives the same one again), and the ones in between are dropped, so the encoder can never send more than it was asked to. The call quality log (and "Copy sharing diagnostics") shows a share that sends more or fewer frames than it was asked for.
